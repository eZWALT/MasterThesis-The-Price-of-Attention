### Disagreements and tensions

1. The Methods says, “the study was not pre-registered, so four labels record the status of each test” (p. 9), while it later says the confirmatory “measure, the contrast, and its weights were fixed on theoretical grounds before the result was read” (p. 9). The Results then calls Table 4 “the confirmatory family” (p. 10). This is a defensible *analysis-status* convention, but it is not preregistration and cannot independently establish that the measure, window, family, or endpoint was specified before researcher discretion. The paper should call these “designated confirmatory analyses” and state what dated protocol fixed them.
2. The paper says in Methods, “each condition window is tiled into 4 s epochs and summarised by the median of a fixed number of epochs whose midpoints lie nearest that condition’s visual onset” (p. 8), but Appendix E specifies “the median of the 37 retained epochs whose midpoints lie nearest that condition’s visual onset” (p. 46). This is not a numerical contradiction, but the body withholds the count that is essential to the confirmatory estimand; it is only recoverable from the appendix. The short-form body should state 37 and why it is fixed.
3. The taxonomy defines explicitness `ϵ` separately from presentation `λ` and says “the present study manipulates presentation while holding appeal fixed” (p. 3), but the actual manipulation also changes the model prompt: “The injector ... prepends this as an extra system message” for implicit ads, whereas “Explicit banners are rendered in the UI and never go through `p_inject`” (Appendix B, p. 24). Thus the format result is a bundled interface-plus-generation intervention, not only a surface-format effect. The Discussion acknowledges `θ`–`λ` confounding but never acknowledges this prompt/reply confound.
4. Table 5 labels RQ1 “Supported” because explicit is above implicit on notice, perceived manipulation, and cued memory, while the RQ wording is “overall experience” (p. 33). The paper does disclose that format is a “bundled presentation-plus-disclosure contrast” (p. 33), but the answer silently narrows “overall experience” to selected outcomes. It should say “supported for the measured notice, pressure, and memory outcomes; not supported for trust or credibility,” rather than presenting RQ1 as unqualifiedly supported.
5. The Discussion says “a conversation with an early advertisement was, on average, a conversation in which participants’ visual attention stayed more engaged with the screen” (p. 14), while the immediately preceding/following text says “timing under condition aggregation is confounded with conversational depth” and “how much of the cell belongs to the insertion is not separable” (p. 14). The latter qualification is correct; the former is too strong and turns a depth-confounded condition-aggregation contrast into an attention interpretation.
6. The Discussion says, “the participants whose visual cortex reacted most to the insertion are the participants who trusted the assistant less” (p. 15). The Results reports a Spearman association between two noisy person-level scores and explicitly says “They are association, not mediation” (p. 10). “Visual cortex reacted” and “trusted less” imply localisation and direction that the design does not establish; this should remain “posterior-alpha response was associated with the trust contrast.”
7. The headline association is adjusted within six pairs for each EEG score, but Table 3 says “the two EEG scores are two families by design” (p. 10). The paper also reports that screening all sixteen measures finds posterior α and absolute α (p. 13). There is no pre-data reason given for treating the two confirmatory EEG scores as separate multiplicity families, and “pooling them into one twelve-test family changes no verdict” is not a substitute for declaring the family before testing. The `ρ=.80` claim should report the pooled 12-test adjustment or explain the prespecified family hierarchy.
8. The Abstract says “the onset-locked posterior α response covaried with the change in reported trust” (p. 2), which is technically an association and matches the Results. The Conclusion says “those whose posterior α fell further ... trusted the assistant less” (p. 16), which gives the same relationship directional language. The Discussion partially corrects this with “the dependence can run either way, or through a third variable” (p. 15), but the Abstract and Conclusion should use the same explicitly non-causal wording.

## 2. Findings

### CRITICAL

**C1. Format is confounded with the assistant’s generated reply.** Location: Appendix B, p. 24: “The injector fills `{products_block}` ... and prepends this as an extra system message for that reply”; “Explicit banners are rendered in the UI and never go through `p_inject`.” Location: Methods, p. 7: “The same product then feeds either the mention or the banner, so presentation and product content are not confounded.” The implicit condition changes the assistant’s natural-language answer by instructing the model to weave a product into it; the explicit condition leaves the reply unmodified and adds a separate UI object. Users therefore receive different conversational content, response length, semantic placement, and likely reading demands, in addition to `λ` and `θ`. Same product identity does not isolate format. This is central to RQ1 and every format claim. Reframe the estimand as the bundled intervention actually delivered, or add an analysis/limitation explicitly attributing the effect to the full mention-versus-banner implementation.

**C2. Dataset B’s pre-onset window is contaminated by the advertisement-specific retrieval delay.** Location: Methods, p. 7: “retrieval runs before generation and adds about 3 s, so the silent wait on each advertised turn is about 6 s”; p. 8: Dataset B is “one 4 s epoch before and one after visual onset.” Location: Limitations, p. 16: “the onset-locked pre-onset window ... sits inside the wait.” The pre-minus-post contrast is therefore not a clean baseline-to-ad response: it compares a pre-window occurring during an ad-specific silent wait with an after-onset window, while the matched no-ad reply has a different latency process. The paper acknowledges the confound but still describes Dataset B as “whether the advertisement produces a transient change” (p. 8) and the association as a response “at the moment the advertisement appears” (p. 15). This can create apparent onset effects and directly affects the strongest EEG association. The confirmatory estimand must be relabelled as an onset/latency-bundled contrast, or a latency-matched control/reanalysis must be provided.

**C3. The strongest EEG timing finding is not identifiable as timing.** Location: Results, p. 12: “the only Holm cell is early minus late on posterior α (−0.22 dB ... Holm p=.0496).” Location: Discussion, p. 14: “A late advertisement sits on a shorter, later turn, however, so timing under condition aggregation is confounded with conversational depth.” The late condition is necessarily on turn 4 and a shorter residual conversation, while early is on turn 2 and leaves more interaction after the ad. A whole-condition median of 37 epochs nearest onset still samples different conversational stages and available windows. The exact boundary p-value cannot support a clean timing/neural-response claim. Make “turn-2 versus turn-4 placement in this four-turn protocol” the estimand, and move the stronger timing language out of the abstract/conclusion.

### MAJOR

**M1. Randomisation and a chi-square balance check do not remove task effects.** Location: Methods, p. 7: “tasks rotate in Latin-square order ... so that across participants task identity is not confounded with condition.” Location: Appendix C.7, p. 36: “with N=54 ... exact balance is not possible”; “Neither the paired t ... nor the mixed-model check enters task or position as a term.” The realised task-by-condition table has cells from 5 to 17, and the chi-square tests only test departure from a uniform count pattern, not whether task difficulty or task-specific product relevance affected outcomes. Randomisation makes the assignment unbiased in expectation; it does not guarantee this realised sample is free of task imbalance. Report task-adjusted or task-stratified sensitivity estimates, or narrow the claim to randomisation-based protection in expectation.

**M2. Arm pooling is under-analysed for a design with materially different measurement contexts.** Location: Methods, p. 6: the arms differ in “recruitment or the EEG recording”; p. 7: crowd participants use “participants’ own devices.” Location: Discussion, p. 16: “The two arms are pooled and differ in age, incentive, and device”; “No between-arm difference ... at raw p<.05.” A raw, underpowered comparison of arms is not a test that the same treatment effect holds across laboratory and Prolific contexts. Arm is not included in the primary behavioural model, and the reported arm table only tests the any-ad contrast, not format, timing, or interaction contrasts. Either model arm × contrast terms as prespecified generalisability checks or describe the pooled estimates as an assumption-dependent average.

**M3. The “confirmatory” status is overstated for an unregistered analysis.** Location: Methods, p. 9: “the study was not pre-registered” but “Confirmatory: the measure, the contrast, and its weights were fixed on theoretical grounds before the result was read.” The paper does not identify an external timestamp, locked protocol, or pre-data record for the exact standardised 37-epoch selection, epoch width, rejection threshold, electrode aggregation, outcome construction, or family partition. Appendix E says the 1,050 μV bound “was set after inspecting the cohort’s epoch-amplitude distribution” (p. 46). Designating an analysis after seeing data is not equivalent to preregistration, even if the final rule was not retuned to p-values. Replace “confirmatory” with “designated primary” where appropriate and state which choices were genuinely fixed before inspection.

**M4. The `ρ=.80` association has a fragile multiplicity and selection story.** Location: Results, p. 13: “Under condition aggregation none ... `ρ=.24` ... Locked to onset, trust covaries with posterior α (`ρ=.80` ... Holm `p=.0004).” Location: Table 3, p. 10: “Holm within the declared six pairs for each EEG score”; “the two EEG scores are two families by design.” With two EEG scores and six behavioural pairs, the declared family structure leaves twelve candidate associations across two families; the rationale for not controlling across the two scores is not presented as a pre-data decision. The subsequent “screening trust against all sixteen onset-locked measures” selects posterior α again, even if labelled wider. At n=18, leave-one-out stability does not provide independent validation. Report the pooled multiplicity adjustment and call the result a candidate association, not evidence of a neural marker.

**M5. The pre/post onset analysis has one observation per cell and uncertain event timing, but some prose reads as a response estimate.** Location: Methods, p. 8: “Dataset B retained all 108 pairs, one window per cell”; “leave-one-out error 0.23 s for banners, 0.43 s for mentions.” Location: Results, p. 12: “the advertisement produces a transient change at the moment it becomes visible.” One four-second spectrum before and one after each event gives no within-cell trial averaging, and the implicit event is reconstructed with larger uncertainty. This is especially problematic for format comparison, which contrasts observed explicit paint timing with reconstructed implicit mention timing. Keep the estimate as a noisy windowed contrast and avoid event-specific physiological language.

**M6. “No effect” claims remain limited by the ordinal, repeated, and partly unreliable outcomes.** Location: Methods, p. 8: “Trust is a single item”; Appendix C, p. 29: “Notice (`α=.61`) and neutrality (`α=.63`) are low”; p. 27: credibility means are around 6.0/7. Location: Discussion, p. 14: “trust did not fall on any planned contrast.” The paper does qualify this with the interval and sample constraint, which is good, but the abstract’s “trust itself did not separate from control” and RQ table’s broad support/not-support labels can still be read as substantive absence. State that the data rule out only effects larger than the displayed intervals under this short session and measurement model; do not treat Holm-null as evidence of no trust cost.

**M7. Repeated exposure, order, and questionnaire cueing are not modelled.** Location: Methods, p. 6: “the five conditions are shuffled independently”; p. 7: each block closes with a “22-item Likert questionnaire.” Location: Limitations, p. 16: “participants ... could anticipate being asked whether the assistant had pushed a product.” Condition and session-position count tables only establish approximate assignment balance; the primary estimator does not include session position, prior advertisements, or condition-order carry-over. The warning that conversations are independent does not prevent learning, demand effects, or repeated-item calibration. Add order/position and prior-ad exposure sensitivity analyses or make the causal interpretation explicitly conditional on no carry-over.

**M8. The headline phrase “attention has a price” is broader than the measured outcomes.** Location: Abstract, p. 2: “Attention has a price, and in a conversation the user pays it.” Location: Results, p. 11: “no participant clicked”; Methods, p. 8: EEG measures are “spectral indices ... not measures of control or attention in themselves.” The strongest behavioural result is perceived commercial pressure, not an attention measure; the EEG timing result is depth-confounded, and the EEG marker is explicitly not attention itself. Use “felt commercial pressure has a measured cost” or otherwise tie “attention” to a clearly operationalised outcome.

### MINOR

**m1. The RQ1 answer is too binary for a multidimensional outcome.** Location: Table 5, p. 33: “RQ1 ... Supported ... trust and credibility Holm-null.” “Supported” should be qualified by the outcomes that moved; otherwise a reader may infer a general experience effect.

**m2. The null interaction is described as additive.** Location: Discussion, p. 14: “the data are consistent with format and timing acting as two additive main effects.” A null interaction is compatible with additivity, but the intervals are not a proof of a practically negligible interaction unless a smallest effect of interest is stated. Replace with “no interaction was detected; the interval includes small interactions.”

**m3. The taxonomy is only partly used after its introduction.** Location: Theory, p. 5: “we propose the provisional organising representation”; Conclusion, p. 16: the full taxonomy is restated. The paper does use `λ`, `θ`, `Π`, and `π` in Methods, but `Γ` and `μ` largely disappear after the theory section. Either shorten the taxonomy or show one concrete analysis/design consequence for each retained component.

**m4. The distinction between notice and detection is handled well but not everywhere maximally sharply.** Location: Methods, p. 8: “brand mention alone is not treated as advertisement detection”; Discussion, p. 13: “An implicit mention therefore reaches a large share of users without being recognised as advertising.” The latter is supported by the sponsored-content item, but the notice composite also includes a sponsored-button item and the no-ad brand item is above midpoint. Keep “sponsored recognition” attached to percentage claims rather than “notice” alone.

**m5. The paper claims a “user-side measurement layer” but reports only post-condition self-report for behavioural cost.** Location: Introduction, p. 2: “we measure what each ... costs the user”; Methods, p. 8: the questionnaire is completed “after each condition.” This is acceptable for the stated study, but “as it is incurred” in the gap paragraph (p. 4) should not imply online or momentary behavioural measurement.

**m6. The short-form body and appendices duplicate some material.** Location: Results, p. 12: “Figure 2 shows the two estimands side by side for all sixteen measures”; Appendix D, pp. 39–41: the same full EEG board and forest statistics are reprinted. The duplication is not fatal, but the body could retain the confirmatory rows and direct readers to the appendix for all exploratory measures.

### VERIFY

**v1. The novelty sentence is not supported by a systematic search.** Location: Related Work, p. 4: “To the best of our knowledge no study sits at the intersection of LLMs, advertising, and EEG.” The citations establish adjacent areas but do not establish an exhaustive literature search. Verify against the final submission date and soften to “we found no...” with search scope, or retain a clearly qualified gap claim.

**v2. Ethics and funding metadata are incomplete in the paper as delivered.** Location: p. 17: “supported by XXXXX under grant number XXXXX”; “approved by XXXXX under approval number XXXXX.” If these are anonymisation placeholders, they must be replaced in the camera-ready version. As written, the ethics provenance and funding disclosure are not publication-ready.

**v3. The platform/release claims need an availability check.** Location: Abstract, p. 2: “An open multimodal dataset”; Data Availability, p. 17: “are released as a raw ... and a processed dataset.” Verify that both links resolve, contain the stated 54/18 participants and required event/onset fields, and include the exact analysis version needed to reproduce Tables 4, 20, and 21. The PDF alone does not give a complete participant-level exclusion ledger or the crowd attention-check rule.

**v4. The related-work claim about “no user-side evidence” is too absolute.** Location: Related Work, p. 4: “the user-side evidence stops at self-report after the fact.” This may be intended as a scope statement, but the cited Tang, Salvi, and native-advertising work appears to include behavioural or recognition measures. Check the references and revise to the narrower claim that prior work does not combine these factors with the paper’s EEG/onset design.

## 3. Top 5 vulnerabilities

1. The implicit-versus-explicit contrast changes the model prompt and the generated reply as well as the UI format and disclosure, so the paper cannot attribute its main format effects to `λ` alone.
2. The Dataset B pre-onset window lies inside an advertisement-specific retrieval delay, making the strongest EEG “onset response” and its trust association potentially latency effects rather than ad-onset physiology.
3. The boundary Dataset A posterior-α result compares turn 2 with turn 4 and is inseparable from conversational depth, residual conversation length, and serial position.
4. The pooled laboratory/Prolific analysis does not model arm-by-treatment effects despite different devices, incentives, settings, and sample compositions; the raw arm check is not an adequate invariance test.
5. The `ρ=.80` result is based on 18 people, two EEG score families, six behavioural pairs per score, and later sixteen-measure screening; its multiplicity and selection rationale are not strong enough for the prominent “cortex reacted ... trusted less” language.

## 4. Three strongest aspects

1. The paper is unusually explicit about estimands: it separates condition aggregation from onset-lock, gives the participant-level contrast equation and weights, and reports both t-based and raw Wilcoxon checks without calling the latter Holm-adjusted.
2. The behavioural reporting is disciplined about post hoc localisation, trust-versus-credibility distinctions, notice versus recognition, and the difference between felt pressure and advertising effectiveness.
3. The EEG appendix gives a genuinely inspectable pipeline: event-clock reconstruction, epoch rejection, ICA thresholds, no-ICA sensitivity, width sensitivity, measure definitions, and complete confirmatory tables rather than only selected p-values.

## 6. Reviewer verdict

I would argue for weak reject unless the authors recast the paper around the bundled interventions actually delivered and materially tighten the EEG claims. The behavioural experiment has a useful user-centric question and careful reporting, but the central format contrast changes the generated assistant response, the onset-locked EEG baseline is contaminated by the advertising retrieval delay, and the headline timing result is confounded with turn/depth. The association is interesting but not yet a validated neural correlate, especially with `n=18` and the unresolved two-score multiplicity choice. If those are transparently reframed, arm/task/order sensitivities are added, and the release/ethics details are completed, the work could become a credible weak accept as a careful first measurement study rather than a causal account of attention.

## 7. Rest of the paper

- **Introduction:** Replace “attention” as the unqualified bill with the measured constructs (felt commercial pressure, trust/credibility, recognition, and spectral indices); make clear that the RQs concern bundled format-plus-disclosure conditions.
- **Related Work:** Narrow the “no user-side evidence” and intersection novelty claims, and state the search boundary.
- **Theory:** Reduce notation that is not used analytically, or explain how `Γ` and `μ` constrain the experiment rather than only defining them.
- **Methods:** State the 37-epoch choice in the main text; report exact attention-check/exclusion rules; add task, session position, prior-ad exposure, and arm sensitivity models; document what was fixed before data inspection.
- **Appendices:** Provide a participant/exclusion ledger and the exact event-latency validation procedure; retain the full EEG tables, but avoid duplicating complete boards already legible in the body.

## Typos and wording

- p. 1, Introduction: “within everybody’s reach” is awkward; “available to nearly everyone” is clearer.
- p. 1, Introduction: “advertising paid for the technologies in which frontier AI emerged” is a broad causal assertion and reads rhetorically rather than evidentially.
- p. 2, Abstract: “the user pays: attention, trust, and the experience” is a mixed metaphor and should name the measured costs directly.
- p. 4, Related Work: “what is missing is the user’s bill measured while it is incurred” is vivid but overstates the timing of the questionnaire and EEG estimands.
- p. 6, Methods: “which is why it cancels in the format and timing contrasts” should be “which is why it is common to those advertised-condition contrasts”; “cancels” is too absolute for a latency that can interact with turn or format.
- p. 11, Results: “the one cell on which the three estimators straddle the threshold” is awkward; “the only planned cell with discrepant threshold decisions across estimators” is more precise.
- p. 14, Discussion: “what stands is the explicit-early response against its own control” is colloquial; “the remaining evidence is the explicit-early versus matched-control contrast” is clearer.
- p. 15, Discussion: “the one place where the two records of this study ... agree on who paid the most” is rhetorical and implies mechanism; replace with a direct association statement.
- p. 16, Conclusion: “counter-intuitively” should be “contrary to the directional expectation” or removed.
- p. 17, Acknowledgements: “even if it’s just a bit” is informal for a conference paper.
- p. 24, Appendix B: “The chat model does not receive a single prompt” is understandable but could be “The chat model receives a concatenation of prompt components.”
- p. 35, Table 14 caption: “Cohort N = 54” is less precise than “Full sample N = 54”; the table then gives missingness only for sex and education.
- p. 46, Appendix E: “the epoch is the epoch” in “here the window is the epoch” is repetitive; rewrite as “each onset window is one 4 s epoch.”
